# Storytelling and retention craft — for talking-head / personal-narrative cuts

Source: **"How Logan Paul's Best Video Was Made | Editing Breakdown"**, HillierSmith
(Hayden Hillier-Smith, the editor of the video he is breaking down) — 13:30, 2022.
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2v-Dv5YYaGQ>. His description credits the cut with
**75% retention**. Transcript pulled with `yt-dlp --write-auto-sub`.

This file is **craft**, not correctness. Nothing here is a Hard Rule. It exists because
the gotchas file is full of things that make a render *valid* and almost nothing about
what makes it *worth watching*, and because this channel's format — one locked-off
camera, one person talking for 13 minutes — is the format where craft carries the entire
load.

**Read the translation section before applying any of it.** He is cutting an adventure
vlog with unlimited b-roll, a hired animator and a drone. We are cutting a confession
filmed at a desk. Several of his techniques are actively wrong here, and the useful
work is knowing which.

---

## 1. The techniques that transfer with no assets at all

These cost nothing but decisions. They are the highest-value part of the video for us.

### Contrast editing — the talking head should sometimes be naked

His opening alternates deliberately: talking head with **no music and no effects at
all**, then a hard cut to something dense with layered sound, then back to naked at the
peak of that energy. His claim is that the contrast is what creates focus — the quiet is
not an absence, it is the thing that makes the loud register.

The trap this diagnoses: **scoring a 13-minute cut wall-to-wall**. A bed that never
leaves stops being an emotional signal and becomes room tone. If the music is always
there, its arrival and departure can no longer mean anything.

### Rhythm variety is structural, not decorative

After about two minutes of fast, aggressive cutting he deliberately drops to a slow
sequence, then builds to a medium pace. His reasoning: sustaining the fast rhythm would
be *exhausting*, and the slow stretch reads as refreshing only because of what preceded
it. Pace is a contrast system, not a setting.

For us: segment length and cut density should differ **per scene, by intent**. A cut
where every scene has the same average segment length is a cut with no rhythm, however
many cuts it contains.

### Hyper-focus: cut a long passage to only its progression lines

Two hours of phone negotiations became a short sequence of only the lines that move the
story, with hard time skips — his phrase is that the edit moves on before the viewer can
settle. This is take selection, and it is free.

Directly relevant to us: a speaker circling the same point three times is not three
beats, it is one beat and two restarts (see also the "cutting a repeated passage" entry
in gotchas.md).

### Withhold the payoff, and let a word drive the cutting

The video's subject is a "rare" card, and he lets that word set the grammar: **elusive,
sneaky, sparse**. So the card is cut to for only a few frames at a time, covered by
headlines, and — at the first apparent reveal — shown as an *animation* rather than the
real object. The real photograph is withheld for the entire opening.

The general form: **decide what the one image of your story is, and do not show it
clearly until it is earned.** Anticipation is the retention mechanism, not payoff.

### The rule of three

Establish a pattern with one, confirm it with a second, and the third lands as a payoff
because the viewer is now predicting it. He uses it on a collection reveal and calls it
shockingly effective for a satisfying cut.

### Music build-and-break on story-changing information

When a fact lands that changes the story, the music builds and then **breaks** — the
break is what marks the beat. He uses it twice in close succession, and again holds on a
shot while the music swells at the moment of a decision, then rewards the decision
immediately with a change of texture and pace. His framing: a change in the narrative is
rewarded with variety.

### Tone is set by the music, and can be deliberately "too serious"

He scored a video about trading cards with a track that reads as high-society period
drama — the same cue used in a *Bridgerton* trailer — and played the subject completely
straight. Taking material more seriously than it superficially deserves is a tone choice
available to any cut, and it costs nothing.

### The zenith — the single most important idea in the video

He proposes (admitting he has no settled name for it) the **"zenith card"**: the most
compelling stories edit toward *one* moment that encapsulates everything — the absolute
peak, the ultimate satisfying cut — and everything before it is context and build.

Two consequences, and the second is the one editors get wrong:

1. **You must know which moment it is before you cut.** If you cannot name it, the cut
   has no shape and every scene competes.
2. **At the zenith, do nothing.** His words: the context has already bought full
   attention, so he lets it play by itself. He notes the cuts there are slow and quiet,
   and that this moment carried the **highest retention in the whole video** — and
   elsewhere that he held 17 seconds without a single cut and retention stayed high.

The corollary for us: the temptation to decorate the emotional peak is exactly backwards.
Graphics, music swells and cutaways belong on the *build*. The peak gets silence, a held
shot, and the viewer's own attention.

### Silence and removal as emphasis

At the top moment he strips the audience out of the mix, drops to slow motion, and lets
a long musical phrase carry it. Removing elements is an emphasis tool on equal footing
with adding them.

## 2. The techniques that need translating for a one-camera essay

### "Story showing, not storytelling"

His strongest rule: do not cut to the speaker *saying* a thing if you can *show* it.
Where the subject describes a population chart, he shows the chart; twenty of something,
he shows twenty; higher value, he throws money on screen. He is explicit that this
b-roll is unashamedly literal, and that literal is fine because it visualises the point.

**We usually cannot do this** — there is no footage of a childhood in Gandhinagar. The
translation is that **a graphic is story showing**. A routine chain assembled as it is
recited, or a number rendered as a figure, does exactly the job he describes, and the
"literal is fine" licence applies to it directly. Where a script beat is *structured* —
a schedule, a measurement, a list, a comparison — it can be shown rather than only heard.

Where a beat is not structured (a feeling, a regret), there is nothing to show, and the
honest answer is to hold on the face.

### Withholding the talking head

He goes a full minute without returning to the speaker, so that when he does, the line
is marked as important by its scarcity alone. He calls the talking head a **bookend** to
the sequence.

We have the inverse problem: the talking head is nearly all we have, so we cannot make it
scarce. **So invert the technique — make the cutaway the scarce thing.** With five or six
cutaways across thirteen minutes, each arrival is an event, and that is a resource to
spend on the lines that deserve marking, not to spread evenly for coverage's sake.

### The emotional line

He draws the narrative as a single line: desire builds, a goal is set, escalation, then
the failure drops the line, "the pit of despair", then the climb back. He argues the
editor's job is to make the audience *fall*, which is why he lets the strings go
melodramatic at the bottom.

This transfers completely — a memoir has this shape natively — but the register must
come down. His melodrama is knowingly silly and he says so. On a first-person account of
real family pressure, the equivalent gesture is restraint: the fall is already in the
words, and the music's job is to not fight them.

## 3. What does NOT transfer — and why

- **The two-minute fast-cut opening.** It works because he has an unlimited b-roll
  library and a subject that rewards velocity. A personal essay opens on a confession;
  cutting a confession fast undercuts it. Ep1's retention export already showed the
  benchmark-relative danger zone was **hedging**, not slowness.
- **Extreme close-up montages** (his Kubrick reference). Needs coverage a single locked
  camera does not have. Faking it with punch-ins on one frame reads as a glitch, not
  obsession — see the coverage/push-in entry in gotchas.md.
- **Custom animated sequences.** He hired a professional animator for his. Our
  equivalent is `helpers/components.py`, which is a different and much smaller tool.
- **Aggressive time-skip cutting of dialogue.** Fine for negotiations on a phone; on a
  first-person account it removes the breathing that makes it sound true.

## 4. Checklist for a talking-head cut

Run before picture lock:

1. **Name the zenith.** One timecode, one sentence. If you cannot, the cut has no shape.
2. **Is the zenith undecorated?** No graphic, no swell landing on it, no cutaway across
   it. Build up to it, then get out of the way.
3. **Does the music ever stop?** If there is no unscored stretch, the score has no
   meaning. At minimum the cold open and the peak should be naked.
4. **Does any music break land on story-changing information?** Pick the two or three
   facts that change the story and mark one of them.
5. **Does pace differ between scenes, on purpose?** Compare mean segment length per
   scene; if they are all equal, the cut has no rhythm.
6. **What is the one image of this story, and is its clear reveal withheld?** Showing it
   plainly in the first minute spends it.
7. **Is there a pattern of three anywhere?** If a motif appears twice, consider a third.
8. **Are the cutaways scarce enough to be events?** Evenly spread coverage is wallpaper.
9. **Is every structured beat shown, not just said?** Schedules, numbers, lists, compares.
10. **Is every unstructured beat left alone?** Feelings do not want graphics.

| Music breaks on story-changing info | **Fail** — beds cross-fade by scene, not by beat |
| The one image is withheld | **Fail** — the clear cricket photo lands at 1:00 |
| The zenith is named | **Fail at lock** — never identified before cutting |
| The zenith is undecorated | **Unknown at lock** — music runs continuously across it |
| Cutaways scarce enough to be events | **Pass** — 6 in 12:55 |

The two failures worth carrying into every future episode of this channel:

- **Name the zenith in the strategy step, before any boundary is chosen.** It belongs
  next to the runtime decision, not after picture lock.
- **Decide the one image and withhold it.** On Ep2 the obvious move was to show the
  cricket photograph obscured or partial early, and give the clear full frame only at
  the moment the story turns.
